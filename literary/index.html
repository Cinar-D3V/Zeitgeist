Literary Analysis

When Franz Kafka wrote In the Penal Colony in October of 1914, Europe had just started a war that many people expected to be short and glorious. Kafka’s story describes something very different. It shows a world where tradition, authority, and technology create senseless cruelty. Through a cold tone, unnamed characters, and the central symbol of the execution machine, Kafka captures the zeitgeist of Europe, where obedience and violence destroyed everything they claimed to protect.

Kafka’s style creates a tone that describes horrible torture in technical detail, as if he were explaining a machine. The officer in the story lists the machine’s parts, like the Bed and the Designer, and calmly explains how they work while someone is waiting to be killed by it. This makes violence seem normal rather than horrible. This style connects to modern war, when killing became an industrial process rather than something about individual lives. None of the characters have names. They are only called by titles such as “the officer,” “the explorer,” and “the condemned man.” This shows how individuals are reduced to roles inside a larger system, just like soldiers during the war.

The apparatus itself is the most powerful symbol in the story. It carves the prisoner’s sentence into his body over twelve hours. The condemned man is a soldier who fell asleep while on duty, and his sentence is “Honor thy superiors!” He was never told what his sentence was, and the officer explains that guilt “is never to be doubted” (Kafka). This shows how the authority in the story does not allow people to question the system or defend themselves. This relates to soldiers, who would get punished for small mistakes and were told to obey without questioning. The machine also represents the technology of WWI. Machine guns, artillery, and poison gas turned killing into something that could happen on a massive scale, where human life became less important than the machines.

The officer believes in the machine almost like a religion. He claims that around the sixth hour, the dying man experiences a moment when he finally understands his sentence. Kafka shows something very different. When the explorer refuses to support the old system, the officer frees the prisoner, straps himself into the machine, and sets it to write “Be just!” Instead of working perfectly, the machine breaks apart and kills him, with no sign of the promised understanding on his face. This shows how the old system the officer believed in eventually destroys the people who support it. It also connects to the fate of Europe during the war. Empires like Austria-Hungary sent millions of people to die believing in honor and duty, but the war destroyed those empires and left people with death and no real meaning behind it.

The explorer adds to Kafka’s criticism. He does not agree with the machine but also takes no action to stop it. In the end, he leaves the island and makes sure the prisoner and soldier cannot follow him. This shows how people can see something wrong happening but still choose to stay out of it. The explorer represents the people who see injustice and then avoid getting involved, allowing the system to continue.

In the Penal Colony shows Kafka’s concerns about the world around him. The faith in tradition, authority, and technology that once gave Europe a sense of order turned into a machine that destroyed the people who believed in it. Written when the war began and published as it ended, Kafka’s story captures a world where obedience and violence destroyed everything people thought they were protecting.
